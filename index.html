Research in motion · Graph Neural Networks

How a graph neural network learns from a network

Graph neural networks learn from data where the connections matter: users and devices in a computer network, roads in a city, regions in an image. Each node starts with its own features. In every layer it gathers messages from its neighbours and updates itself, so information spreads one hop further. The learned embeddings separate the normal groups and expose the rare anomalies, the imbalanced node classification problem at the heart of Dr Aajay's GATE-GNN, CL3E-GNN, PIMPC-GNN and GANT-GNN models. A curious researcher watches through his lens. Click any node to follow its messages.

Research in motion · Spatio-temporal learning

How a spatio-temporal model forecasts traffic flow

Road sensors record traffic every five minutes. The model learns how congestion spreads across connected roads (space) and which past moments matter (time), then forecasts the next 30 minutes for every sensor and checks the forecast against what actually happens. This is the idea behind Dr Aajay's forecasting models, including CHAMFormer, ST-FSFusion, MAST-Net, MCPST and WaveFSL, which also transfer what they learn to cities with little historical data. Research in motion · Medical image processing

How AI reads medical images

Two scans carry different information: MRI shows anatomy, PET shows function. The model fuses them across scales, segments the region of interest with an encoder–decoder network, and combines the result with patient data in a report built on the FUTURE-AI principles for trustworthy healthcare AI. The steps mirror Dr Aajay's work on FAMAFuse for multimodal image fusion, MDPNet for polyp segmentation and the FUTURE-AI international guideline in The BMJ. Images and patient data are simulated.

Dr Abdul Joseph Fofanah, known as Dr Aajay, is an AI and deep learning researcher, university educator and the founder of the Centre for Research, Innovation and Development (CRID-SL). His work sits where machine learning meets real-world systems. He builds graph neural networks, attention and Transformer models for relational data that changes over time, and applies them to cybersecurity, transport, healthcare and public health.

A teacher first 2005–2015

He trained as a teacher, earning a Higher Teacher Certificate in Mathematics and Computer Science from Milton Margai College of Education and Technology, followed by a BSc (Hons) and later an MSc in Computer Science from Njala University. He began lecturing in 2010, teaching statistics, data mining, pattern recognition, data structures and programming at Njala University and Milton Margai Technical University. He led curriculum review for the Department of Mathematics and Computer Science and was appointed Senior Lecturer in 2022, a rank validated by Sierra Leone's Tertiary Education Commission.

Data on the front line 2015–2023

During the West African Ebola epidemic he joined the UN Migration Agency (IOM) as Senior Data Analyst and Information Management Officer, taking a leading data management role in the response across Sierra Leone, Liberia and Guinea. That work earned him the IOM Excellence Award in 2017. Over eight years with IOM he led population mobility mapping and displacement tracking in Kenya, Nepal, Afghanistan, Thailand and Myanmar. He designed databases and GIS products, built dashboards, wrote training-of-trainers curricula and worked alongside national governments and Red Cross societies, including on Nepal's COVID-19 response.

In parallel he ran an independent IT consultancy in Freetown. It built the primary data centre for Skye Bank and a secondary data centre for Access Bank (SL), delivered websites for UNICEF and Government of Sierra Leone ministries, and led the design of the national Automated Vehicle and Driver Licence System.

Into research 2018–2026

A Chinese Government Scholarship took him to Nankai University, where he completed a Master of Engineering in Software Engineering and published early work on protecting medical images through watermarking. An Australian Government Research Training Program Scholarship then brought him to Griffith University in Brisbane. His doctoral thesis, Spatio-Temporal Feature Learning: From Traffic Flow Prediction to Imbalanced Graph Anomaly Detection for Cybersecurity, developed a family of models that learn from networks whose structure and signals evolve over time. In 2025 he received the Jeffrey Blee Memorial Prize for higher degree research excellence. Throughout his candidature he taught as a Teaching Fellow in Griffith's School of ICT and became an Associate Fellow of Advance HE. He also completed a second doctorate, a PhD in Data Science and Health Informatics at Northcentral University in California, which grounds his work on health information systems and medical AI.

Research today

Dr Aajay has published 16 peer-reviewed journal articles, 12 as first author, in venues including IEEE Transactions on Neural Networks and Learning Systems, Information Fusion, IEEE Transactions on Cybernetics and The BMJ, where he co-authored the FUTURE-AI international guideline for trustworthy healthcare AI. His work has been cited more than 1,450 times. He has written four books, on research methodology, machine learning, database technologies and watermarking, and has reviewed over 100 manuscripts for Springer Nature, Elsevier, IEEE and PeerJ.

His current focus is interaction-aware models for topic and sentiment analytics, and LLM applications built with retrieval, agents, evaluation and guardrails. In the longer term he aims to lead an interdisciplinary programme in trustworthy AI and supervise the next generation of research students. Through CRID-SL he brings these methods back to the institutions and communities where his career began.

Forthcoming book · Generis Publishing

The Chaos of Reason

Finding Order with Game Theory and Complexity

What if the most rational strategy is to stop trying to control the game?

Classical game theory imagines perfectly rational players, complete information and stable rules. Real life offers none of these. From classrooms and elections to financial markets, cyber conflict and war, the systems we live in are adaptive, networked and constantly changing. In The Chaos of Reason, Dr Aajay brings game theory and complexity science together to show why strategies built on prediction and control so often fail, and what works instead.

Drawing on cases from Sierra Leone, West Africa and around the world, from the resistance of Bai Bureh to the forgotten pioneers of wireless communication, he argues for a new science of strategy, in which leaders act less like chess masters and more like gardeners, cultivating the conditions in which order can emerge. Rigorous yet accessible, and grounded in his own journey from the streets of Freetown to doctoral research in artificial intelligence, it is a book for policymakers, educators, researchers and anyone trying to make good decisions in an unpredictable world.
